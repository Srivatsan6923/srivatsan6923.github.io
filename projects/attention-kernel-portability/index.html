Does the fastest attention kernel survive a change of GPU?

Prefill winners change between GPUs far more often than decode winners, and much of the difference traces back to how each library was built

At batch 4 and 256 tokens, PyTorch's memory-efficient attention was the clear prefill winner on an A100. I ran the same call on an H100 and it took 125 µs. cuDNN did the same work there in 36.

So I measured how often that happens. I timed every attention backend a PyTorch serving stack can reach, from FlashAttention-2 and 3 to FlashInfer, cuDNN and Triton, on six NVIDIA GPUs from Ampere to Blackwell, for both prefill and decode. When a shape has a clear winner on two GPUs, the prefill winner changes about 4 times in 10. The decode winner changes about once in 10.

Context

Why the choice matters

vLLM and SGLang both pick an attention backend from a built-in preference order that depends mainly on the GPU generation, and both let you override it (VLLM_ATTENTION_BACKEND, --attention-backend). Someone wrote that order after benchmarking on the GPUs they had. When the fleet changes, the order usually doesn't.

Attention runs once per layer for every step, so small per-call gaps add up fast. The numbers below are two measured calls on an H100, multiplied out for a 32-layer model.

cuDNN1.1 ms
Memory-efficient4.0 ms

Decode · 16K context, batch 1

FlashInfer27.5 ms

Attention time per generated token. FlashInfer is the fastest decode backend on about half the shapes on this GPU, but not on this one.

To see how often gaps like these appear, I ran the same grid of shapes on six GPUs.

Prefill covers 256 to 8,192 tokens; decode adds one token to a KV cache of 512 to 16,384. Around those I varied batch, head layout (full multi-head and grouped-query), head dimension, BF16 and FP16, causal masking, eager launch against CUDA Graphs, and a warm or cold L2. That gives 159 prefill and 244 decode shapes per GPU. Every GPU ran the same software: PyTorch 2.9 on CUDA 12.8, Triton 3.5, flash-attn 2.8.3 and FlashInfer 0.6.18, plus FlashAttention-3 built for the H100. Each comparison is between backends on the same GPU, never between GPUs, because the question is which kernel to run, not which GPU is faster.

Ground rules

Most benchmark winners are ties

A benchmark table always has a fastest row. If the top two backends are 3% apart and run-to-run noise is 5%, that row is a coin flip, and coin flips change between GPUs all the time. Treating those as winner changes would mostly measure noise.

So I only call a winner when it leads the runner-up by at least 10%, about the smallest gap I'd change a production config for, and when a 95% bootstrap interval on that lead stays above 1.

By that standard, most shapes have no winner. About 29% of prefill shapes and 34% of decode shapes have a clear one, and the typical lead of the fastest backend over the second is only 5 to 6%. Nudging the margin to 5% or 15% moves those shares by about ten points either way.

The main result

Prefill winners change between GPUs far more often than decode winners

For each shape with a clear winner on two different GPUs, I checked whether it was the same backend. I pooled the ten pairs among the A100, A10, L40, L40S and H100. The RTX 5090 gets its own row, for a reason the decode section explains.

A raw diff of two benchmark tables makes both regimes look unstable, with the winner changing for over half of shapes, but most of those changes are between backends that were tied to begin with. With ties removed, prefill still changes in 42% of comparisons, and decode drops to 10%.

I wanted to rule out FlashAttention-3 as the cause, since it runs only on the H100, so I recomputed every pair using only backends both GPUs could run. The prefill rate stayed where it was. Prefill rankings move even among libraries every GPU has.

The upgrades people actually make are where prefill moves most. Between the L40 and the H100, all 8 shapes with a clear winner on both GPUs changed winner, and the same was true for all 4 between the A100 and the H100. Decode between the Ampere and Ada GPUs agreed almost everywhere.

Practical impact

The cost of reusing a choice

A changed winner only matters if the old choice is noticeably slower. To measure that, I took the backend that won on one GPU, ran it on another, and divided by the fastest time on the second GPU. I did this for every ordered pair of the six GPUs and every shape with a clear winner on the first. Most of those shapes didn't change winner at all, so the result is close to what you'd see by copying a config to a new GPU.

The median reused choice costs nothing, so a default usually carries over fine. The losses sit in the tail. More than a quarter of reused prefill choices are over 10% off the best, and the worst one in twenty is 1.6× or more. Decode is tighter, at about one in seven over 10% and 1.3× for the worst one in twenty.

The darkest prefill cells all land on the H100. Choices made on the A100, A10 or L40 run there at up to 3.5× the best time. These are short prompts, mostly 256 to 1,024 tokens, where the older GPU's winner was one of PyTorch's SDPA paths and the H100's best was cuDNN at about 36 µs. Prompts that short are common in chat turns, so the gap shows up directly in time to first token.

The darkest decode cells start on the RTX 5090. FlashInfer isn't available there in this stack, and on a few small batch-8 shapes the plain PyTorch implementation ends up fastest. Carried to an L40S or H100, that choice runs 5 to 9× slower than the best.

Some choices can't be reused at all. Most clear prefill winners on the H100 are FlashAttention-3, which doesn't run on the other five GPUs, and most decode choices that can't move are FlashInfer picks that don't run on the RTX 5090. Each accounts for about one in seven reused choices in its regime. I left these out of the cost figures rather than substituting the new GPU's best time, since the fix for a backend that's missing is different from the fix for one that's slow.

Mechanism · prefill

Why prefill moves: the fast kernels are built for specific GPUs

Prefill is matrix-multiply work, and the fastest prefill kernels are written around one tensor-core generation. Figure 7 plots each backend relative to the fastest one at every length; switch GPUs to compare.

On the H100, two different limits decide the order. From 256 to 1,024 tokens, FlashAttention-3's time barely moves (84, 124, 108 µs) while the work grows sixteen-fold. A flat curve like that means a fixed per-call cost is setting the time, and cuDNN's fixed cost is lower: 36 µs at 256 tokens. Once there is enough work to fill the GPU, the kernel written for Hopper's asynchronous loads and warpgroup MMA takes over, and FlashAttention-3 leads from 1,024 tokens up. On the A10 and the Ada GPUs, the order is different again, and that is where the library build comes in.

I listed the GPU code shipped inside each installed library with cuobjdump. CUDA lets a GPU run code compiled for an older GPU of the same generation, so an A10 or an L40 can run kernels built for the A100. They run fine, but they were tuned for different hardware.

The winners follow this table closely. FlashAttention-2 wins about half the prefill shapes on the A100, where its code is native, and almost none on the A10 and Ada GPUs, where it runs A100 code. Decode makes the point more sharply. PyTorch's SDPA decode path runs PyTorch's own build of the FlashAttention-2 kernels, so SDPA against flash-attn is the same algorithm in two different builds. Where both builds are native, flash-attn wins nearly every time. Where only PyTorch's is native, SDPA wins half to three quarters of the time.

Triton is consistent with this too. It compiles every kernel for the exact GPU at run time, so its code is always native. It is one of the two most frequent prefill winners on the A10 and both Ada GPUs, and wins nothing on the A100, where FlashAttention-2 is native. The direct test is to rebuild flash-attn with A10 and Ada targets and time it again, and that is the first experiment on my list. In practice, "FlashAttention-2 is faster" is a claim about one build on one GPU, so a benchmark report should name the build as well as the version.

Mechanism · decode

Why decode holds: every kernel is racing the same memory wall

Decode is the opposite workload. One new token per sequence reads the whole KV cache, about 2 · B · Hkv · N · D · s bytes per step, and does very little math with it. Once the batch is large enough to fill the GPU, every good decode kernel is limited by the same thing, memory bandwidth, and that is why their ranking moves so much less.

Across the whole decode grid, FlashInfer is the fastest backend on half to two thirds of shapes on the A10, A100 and H100, with FlashAttention-2's KV-cache kernel taking most of the rest. The slice above uses grouped-query attention with 8 KV heads, where FlashAttention-2 does better, especially on the H100. Most of the map is faded because in decode the top backends are usually within a few percent of each other.

Shape still matters inside one GPU. At batch 1 on the H100, FlashAttention-2's KV-cache kernel takes about 102 µs at every length, a fixed cost rather than a bandwidth limit. FlashInfer is faster at 512 tokens and grows in step with the cache, to 860 µs at 16K. At batch 32 the GPU is full, every kernel is streaming memory, and the gap closes: FlashAttention-2 reads at an effective 2.49 TB/s at 16K, 83% of what a plain copy achieves on that H100, which leaves little room for any backend to pull ahead.

The RTX 5090: when the usual winner isn't there

Decode rankings shift most on the RTX 5090, and the reason is availability, not speed. FlashInfer compiles its kernels at run time against the installed CUDA toolkit, and under the CUDA 12.8 toolkit in this stack it didn't run on the 5090's sm_120. Every precompiled library ships sm_120 code and ran normally. Since FlashInfer is the most common decode winner on four of the other five GPUs, any pair that includes the 5090 changes decode winner about three times in four. FlashAttention-2's KV-cache kernel takes its place, winning 137 of 192 decode shapes on the 5090.

Practice

What I'd change in a serving stack

If I were setting attention backends for a fleet with more than one GPU type, this is what I'd do.

  1. Tune prefill per GPU type, once, at deploy time. This is where winners move and where the reuse tail reaches 1.6×. A short sweep over the prompt lengths you actually serve is cheap next to that, and the engines already have the override flag.

  2. Keep the decode default, and smoke-test it on every new GPU and toolkit. Decode choices travel well. The thing that breaks them is a backend that isn't there, so check that the preferred one loads before trusting the order.

  3. Split prefill by prompt length. On one H100 the best backend changes between short and long prompts, so a single per-GPU entry leaves time on the table at one end. Two entries cover it.

  4. Run cuobjdump on the wheels you ship. It takes a minute and tells you whether a library is native on each GPU in the fleet, which explains rankings that otherwise look random.

  5. Require a real gap before changing a config. Most apparent winner changes in a raw benchmark diff are ties. Run several processes, pin them to the GPU's NUMA node, and look for 10% with an interval clear of 1.

Method

Making the numbers trustworthy

Every GPU here was a rented cloud machine, so getting repeatable numbers took some work. The most useful technique came from the H100, a two-socket host with 208 cores. Timings inside one process were tight, but separate processes disagreed more than they should have. Each launch landed on whichever CPU socket the scheduler picked, and a process on the far socket pays an extra hop on every kernel launch and pinned-memory copy. Pinning each process to the CPUs of the GPU's own NUMA node fixed it.

A check for exclusive GPU access would never have caught this, because the GPU really was idle and exclusive. Only running every shape in five separate processes exposed it, which is also why the confidence intervals resample whole processes rather than single calls.

Two other checks back every number on this page. Each backend's output was compared against a reference implementation, and every one matched. And I recorded which kernels actually launched: in 99.5% of the runs I traced, the kernel family that ran was the one requested, so each latency belongs to the kernel it is labelled with. Each GPU ran on its own machine, so every comparison here is a ranking within one GPU, which is also how a serving team chooses a backend.

Appendix

Methods

Timing

For each shape, backends run in a random order. Each backend is timed in blocks of about 200 µs of back-to-back calls with CUDA events, and the block time is divided by the number of calls. Latency is the median over blocks in a process, then the median over five separate processes. Cold-cache shapes flush L2 before each block. CUDA-graph shapes are timed by graph replay. Confidence intervals use a 2,000-draw bootstrap over whole processes.

Definitions

With Tg,c,b the median latency of backend b on GPU g and shape c, and b1, b2 the fastest and second-fastest backends:

Rg,c = Tg,c,b₂ / Tg,c,b₁     clear winner ⇔ Rg,c ≥ 1.10 and L95(Rg,c) > 1

The winner-change rate is the share of shapes, with a clear winner on both GPUs of a pair, whose winners differ. The cost of reusing GPU g's winner on GPU h is:

Cg→h,c = Th,c,b* / minb Th,c,b,   with b* the winner on g
